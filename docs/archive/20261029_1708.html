Abstract
Relations compose through the entity they share - Alice is Bob's mother and Bob is Carol's father, so Alice is Carol's grandmother - and the order matters, since a father's mother is not a mother's father; verifying a composed relation means closing the loop. We show that if each entity's coordinate frame may be rotated independently (per-entity frame freedom), three things follow. In the pure-gauge sector, relations that share no cycle cannot interact at all. The only gauge-invariant score of a triangle is a class function of its holonomy $H = T_{ij} T_{jk} T_{ki}$ - a Wilson loop. And the only equivariant update of an edge is $U = Ψ(H)\, T_{ij}$ with $Ψ$ conjugation-equivariant. Edge attention by Wilson loops is therefore not one design among many but the unique form the symmetry permits. Three communities hold pieces of this form: the commuting-operator embeddings of knowledge graphs are its abelian (commuting) sector; AlphaFold's triangle update and the Edge Transformer have its shape - an edge updated through the third vertex - without its constraints; and lattice QCD builds it equivariantly on lattices. We instantiate the equivariant sub-family on general relational graphs as holonomy attention. With no training and composition alone, it infers unobserved $S_4$ relations on sparse graphs at 0.88 with a fifth of the edges corrupted, at or slightly below specialist synchronization algorithms, where every commutative architecture is capped at 0.083; its holonomy score flags corrupted triangles at AUC 0.99 where commuting scores are capped at 0.76 by a second selection rule, and it separates clusters without labels.

I-HITL: Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop
Authors: Samuel Breckenridge, Dani Vilardell, Wenhao Wang, Fan Zhang, Ari Juels
First: 2026-10-26T17:52:14+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-27T17:41:57+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Human oversight is a pillar of AI safety. It is often exercised by a human in the loop (HITL) who approves critical actions taken by an AI agent. AI agents' increasing ability to bypass guardrails, though, raises the question of how to enforce HITL robustly. We introduce the concept of inalienable human-in-the-loop (I-HITL). An I-HITL protocol ensures that an agent can initiate an action only with explicit human approval, even when the human interacts with a system (e.g., a local agentic harness) compromised by the agent. A strong I-HITL protocol additionally ensures such approval even when an adversarial human attempts to delegate approvals to an agent. To motivate I-HITL, we report on experiments with a popular agentic wallet platform in which raising an agent's spending cap requires HITL approval via emailed one-time passcodes (OTPs). Given access to the user's email and a task blocked by the cap, we find that ten different frontier models will often autonomously raise the cap, harvesting OTPs to bypass a HITL guardrail and surfacing other vulnerabilities. We formally define I-HITL using the concept of communication topologies and prove necessary and sufficient conditions for its realization. We show that an I-HITL protocol requires a trusted entity - a trusted computing device (or equivalent) - and characterize which such devices can and cannot realize an I-HITL protocol. We present a prototype I-HITL system called Countersign that can plug into and strengthen existing HITL systems using off-the-shelf consumer mobile devices.

Efficient Token-Level Policy Optimization for Masked Diffusion Models via Velocity Alignment

Abstract
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) successfully aligns autoregressive models in rigorous domains like mathematical reasoning and code generation. However, applying RLVR to Diffusion Large Language Models (dLLMs) is hindered by intractable sequence-level likelihoods, forcing existing methods to rely on unstable, high-variance approximations. We propose Sequence Likelihood-Free Policy Optimization (LFPO), an efficient framework that circumvents this bottleneck by formulating policy alignment as discrete velocity alignment. Inspired by continuous flow matching, LFPO eschews trajectory-level estimation and directly optimizes denoising logits via contrastive token-level updates, providing a stable, low-variance surrogate. Furthermore, predicting terminal solutions from intermediate states implicitly straightens the generation trajectory, enabling high-quality sampling with significantly fewer denoising iterations. Extensive experiments on reasoning and coding benchmarks demonstrate that LFPO matches or exceeds the accuracy of likelihood-based baselines while substantially accelerating inference.

Abstract
Clinical research over electronic health records depends on derived variables whose definitions sit outside the schema, in item codes, time windows, missingness rules, and output-grain choices that change cohorts and downstream conclusions. We introduce M4Bench, 28 clinical derivation tasks across 15 task families on MIMIC-IV and eICU, to test whether AI coding agents can reproduce such derivations and how clinician-reviewed agent skills change their results. Its controls decompose the value of skills into specification completeness, delivery format, and schema familiarity. Skills are released as a versioned, clinician-reviewable markdown delivery format, with a family-level reference-validity audit. M4Bench releases tasks, skills, evaluation code, and sanitized per-run evidence as an open-source benchmark.

Abstract
Existing code generation benchmarks primarily evaluate functional correctness, with limited focus on code efficiency and often restricted to a single language like Python. To address this gap, we introduce EffiBench-X, the first multi-language benchmark designed to measure the efficiency of LLM-generated code. Abstract
A software feature usually evolves over time: developers extend its first version as new use cases and requirements arrive, and each extension builds on code written earlier. Most feature benchmarks evaluate a single request on a fixed codebase. Feature evolution requires evaluating successive extensions on an agent's own earlier code and checking whether the feature remains correct. We introduce FeatureArc to evaluate coding agents on evolving features, together with a pipeline that constructs its tasks from repository histories. The benchmark combines dependencies between successive stages of the same feature, explicit requirements and interface contracts, and executable checks across four programming languages. The pipeline validates fail-to-pass (F2P) tests for new behavior and pass-to-pass (P2P) tests for existing behavior on the reference code before and after each stage. We evaluated 10 coding agents on 73 manually reviewed tasks of 2 to 10 stages. Each agent works in one persistent session and workspace without external test feedback or rollback. The evaluation reveals a gap between first-stage and whole-feature success: pooled success falls from 62.7% at the first stage to 28.5% when the whole feature is tested on the final code. Trajectory analysis identifies inherited defects as a common failure mechanism, present in 72.9% of failed later stages. These earlier errors often remain undetected: in stages following a failure, agents leave the earlier error unnoticed about 95% of the time, and their self-tests rarely revisit earlier stages. Evolving a feature autonomously, without human checks between stages, therefore remains a substantial challenge for current coding agents. At Tencent, the pipeline extends internal evaluations to this setting and guides the development of Hunyuan, Tencent's family of large language models.

Clearing the FoG: Training KG Agents to Retrieve the Evidence Behind Their Answers
Authors: Zongyuan Sun, Shuai Zhao, Yingjun Du, Ke Shu, Ziyue Zhu, Haoran Luo

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can answer many factual questions correctly, but a correct answer is not necessarily a supported one. For an LLM agent, a knowledge graph (KG) plays two roles: as a source of answers, it supplies facts that the model lacks; as a source of evidence, it supplies the supporting triples that make an answer traceable and auditable. However, existing KG agents treat the KG only as a source of answers: their training data carries answers but not evidence, and their training rewards check the answer but not the retrieved evidence, so agents often answer correctly without retrieving the supporting triples. We propose FoG, a framework that trains KG agents to retrieve the evidence behind their answers. FoG consists of evidence-grounded synthesis, a KG interaction agent, and an evidence-coverage process reward (ECPR): the synthesis recursively hides explicit entities behind verifiable sub-questions, so that every question carries its supporting triples by construction; the agent records the triples returned by the KG; and ECPR credits each supporting triple to the turn that first retrieves it. Experiments on three KGQA benchmarks demonstrate that FoG enables 3B--4B open-source agents to outperform substantially larger baselines in answer accuracy and evidence coverage, with zero-shot cross-KG transfer.

Abstract

Abstract

Abstract
Language model agents are post-trained and deployed through a harness, the prompts, tools, and control logic that present a task to the model and execute its outputs. Harnesses are often revised and judged by how an already trained model performs under them, which assumes that what the model learned under one harness carries over to the next. We ask what a policy learns from its harness and what survives when the harness changes. Under KL-regularized control, we show that the log odds of an optimal policy recover how strongly its harness favors each action over the others, so a trained policy carries the preferences of its harness. Deployed return then equals the ceiling of the harness, the best return it offers, minus the mismatch of the policy, the return lost to its own suboptimal decisions. Training changes only the mismatch, while changing the harness moves both terms. Comparing harnesses with a fixed model therefore mixes what each harness offers with how well that model uses it. In MiniWoB++, post-training makes a model's preferences specific to its training harness. Reversing the order of the observation fields leaves every optimal decision unchanged yet lowers a trained model's success from 85.0% to 19.2%. Conversely, every new harness that a fixed-model comparison rejects but that raises the ceiling outperforms the original agent after further training under it, and ceiling and mismatch measured beforehand predict these gains. For coding-agent harnesses on Terminal-Bench Lite, ceiling minus mismatch estimated with a stronger model as a stand-in for the optimum reproduces the accuracy ranking, while either term alone does not. Abstract
Long-horizon coding tasks contain nested subtasks. Typically, agents record interactions sequentially into a growing history, causing context inflation and performance degradation. Compressing or trimming the history without explicit subtask boundaries can discard information needed by unfinished subtasks. Conversely, recursive subagent delegation isolates completed subtasks through child agents by assigning every subtask boundary to delegation, which conflates subtask granularity with executing agent choice, adding execution and coordination overhead. We introduce Spine, an interface and runtime that align model context with subtask nesting and completion. An agent declares explicit subtask boundaries through work scopes, allowing the same agent to execute nested subtasks inline while reserving delegation for independent parallel tasks. The runtime organizes execution in SpineTree, a persistent tree recording subtask relations, local interactions, completion states, and returned results. Before each model call, it compiles the tree into model context just in time, retaining unfinished subtasks' interactions and replacing completed subtasks' details with their results, while preserving prefix-cache reuse. We integrate Spine into Codex to build SpineCodex. With GPT-5.6 Sol, compared with native Codex, SpineCodex improves SWE-Milestone Score by 11.8% while reducing total cost by 32.4%, improves the average ProgramBench test-pass rate by 17.3%, and improves Mean@5 on eight of the ten evaluated FrontierSWE tasks. On SWE-Milestone, it also achieves a comparable Score to Recursive Subagent at 73.7% lower total cost. We also integrate Spine into Pi through a reusable software package: with Gemini 3.8 Flash and Qwen 3.8 27B, it improves SWE-Milestone Score by 9.5-17.5% and the average ProgramBench test-pass rate by 4.3-8.7% compared with native Pi.

Abstract

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly deployed in scientific research, where reliability is critical and the underlying knowledge is densely interconnected. In such settings, hallucinations are particularly damaging: a single erroneous claim on a foundational concept can propagate through multi-step reasoning and corrupt entire trajectories. Existing hallucination benchmarks largely operate at the surface level, treating facts in isolation and relying on uniform accuracy metrics that ignore this topological structure. We address this gap with SCHEMA, the first evidence-grounded, topology-aware evaluation framework for hallucinations in scientific agents.
