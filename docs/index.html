One Policy for 100 Household Tasks: Our Solution for the 2026 BEHAVIOR Challenge
Authors: Ilia Larchenko, Gleb Zarin, Atindra Nair, Keerti Manney, Vitaly Kleban, Mirko Kiefer, Anjana Yodaiken

Abstract
We describe our solution for the 2026 BEHAVIOR Challenge: 100 long-horizon household tasks in the OmniGibson simulator. We placed 9th on the final leaderboard, the only independent team in the top 10. The core of the solution is a single vision-language-action (VLA) model, one checkpoint for all 100 tasks, built on our 1st-place 2025 solution, which is based on $π_{0.5}$. On the 50 tasks shared with 2025, its Q rises from 0.260 to 0.371; across all 100 tasks it scores 0.392. Our main conclusion from 2025 was that the model was undertrained, so most of the 2026 work went into training the model for longer, optimizing some training parameters, making each training step slightly faster and reducing the gradient noise: a migration from 50 to 100 tasks (semantic stages built from the demonstrations' skill annotations, task and stage embeddings of new tasks warm-started from similar old ones, and a 21-dimensional action space matching the teleoperation controls); variance-reduced multi-sample flow matching with a simple noise-vs-compute model that picks the number of action-expert samples per observation without ablations; and a 200k-step training run, about one pass over the 211M frames of the demonstrations. A cloud evaluation infrastructure keeps a shared record with provenance, episode diagnostics and restorable simulator states, and runs six simulators on one GPU together faster than real time. On top of the learned policy, 14 tasks are routed to a GPT-6 Astra coding agent that controls the robot through observation and motion tools, starting every episode from a frozen copy of its own practice session on training instances. Across these 14 tasks, Astra's mean Q is 0.639, against 0.108 for the learned policy. The public submission included only part of the Astra results and scored 0.446 Q with 27.3% full-task success.

Evaluating LLM-Generated World Models for Neuro-Symbolic Reasoning

Abstract
Formal world models can support planning, verification, and safety reasoning, but constructing them from open-ended natural-language situations is difficult, particularly in multi-agent settings where several formalizations may be plausible. We study whether five LLMs can generate such world models as partially observable stochastic games and whether mechanical validity is sufficient to establish that the resulting model faithfully represents the intended decision problem. We introduce a benchmark of 44 scenarios spanning game, situational, and cultural settings and evaluate generated world models along three dimensions: mechanical validity, reference-relative structural agreement, and semantic criteria. Across three empirical tracks, we analyze 410 first-pass generations. In the reference-backed game track, 134/135 generated world models pass a strict mechanical audit, yet mean permutation-invariant structural F1 is only 0.366, with just 28/134 mechanically valid models exactly matching the authored reference. In the cultural track, explicitly naming the country changes the generated structural representation for most LLMs while producing only small changes in automated semantic scores. These results show that executability is a necessary but insufficient criterion for LLM-generated world models: models that are mechanically valid can still differ substantially in the structure used by downstream neuro-symbolic reasoning and planning.

Abstract
Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs) are well known for fast text generation via parallel decoding. However, independent parallel sampling can commit to inconsistent predictions, and less is known about how to selectively revise these early token commitments. In this work, we introduce $\underline{\mathrm{P}}$lanned $\underline{\mathrm{U}}$nmasking-and-$\underline{\mathrm{R}}$evision Joint $\underline{\mathrm{L}}$earning (PURL) for MDMs, a training-based framework that seamlessly unifies parallel generation with selective token revision. We derive a normalized joint transition whose likelihood factorizes into edit selection, unmask selection, and token prediction. These components are trained jointly on partially masked sequences constructed according to the model's own unmasking policy. During inference, all three decisions are supported by a single shared forward pass, enabling the model to decode masked tokens and revise noisy tokens. Across logic puzzles, mathematical reasoning, and code generation, PURL improves accuracy at matched unmask budgets, with relative gains up to 47.4% accuracy improvement and up to an $8\times$ reduction in scheduled decoding rounds. Beyond these gains, our analysis further reveals general principles of revisable decoding: token edits become more effective as context accumulates; early edits can steer unfinished trajectories, whereas later edits tend to remain localized; and effective edits therefore depend on both calibrating when to intervene and learning where and how to revise. Together, these suggest that effective parallel generation depends not on revising more tokens, but on making the right commitments revisable at the right time.

Abstract
Rapidly evolving LLM architectures and heterogeneous mobile hardware make supporting new models in on-device inference engines a recurring and costly system challenge. Each model may require coordinated changes across conversion, graph construction, operators, and hardware backends. Existing coding agents often miss these cross-layer dependencies and struggle to diagnose failures across host--device execution paths. We present EngineAgent, an agentic system for automating on-device LLM model enablement. Its key insight is to separate reusable engine and hardware knowledge from model-specific implementation. Before enablement, EngineAgent constructs a hierarchy-aware engine index that captures engine abstraction layers and cross-layer dependencies, and pre-validates backend-specific build, execution, and debugging workflows using already supported models. For each new model, a builder uses this knowledge to coordinate cross-layer changes, while an auditor checks implementation completeness and drives iterative debugging through device-side validation. Across 12 tasks spanning three mobile LLMs and four engine--backend pairs on CPUs, GPUs, and NPUs, EngineAgent achieves 75% end-to-end success with either of two underlying LLMs, versus 33.3% for the strongest coding-agent baseline. Success requires compilation, on-device execution, and output-logit agreement with reference implementations. EngineAgent also reduces average per-task time and model-API cost relative to baselines using the same underlying LLMs.

Abstract
Software agents built on large language models generate traffic unlike human web traffic, yet routers have no class for it and cannot verify a sender's claim to be an agent. This paper proposes the Verifiable Agentic Traffic Class (VATC), which combines a reserved Differentiated Services Code Point (DSCP) value declaring agent traffic, a router-side classifier that demotes flows contradicting the declaration to a class served after best-effort traffic, and a rate-limited priority queue. VATC was compared with static Differentiated Services (DiffServ), a dedicated class that trusts the marking, and fq_codel, a per-flow fair-queuing scheduler, in two simulators and on a real Linux kernel. VATC matched DiffServ under steady load. In a constructed burst it cut mean agent queuing delay by 98% relative to DiffServ in simulation (assuming an immediate verdict), at a 12% increase in human delay; most of the gain came from the separate class. Under a spoofed flood in the kernel, the rate limit held human latency 11 times below DiffServ's. Without verification, DiffServ and a tightly configured fq_codel gave legitimate agents lower latency than VATC; a live four-feature classifier then demoted all 20 bulk-shaped spoofers and gave agents 0.30 ms, but missed six spoofers that copied the agent pattern. With emulated network delay, a timing feature of the classifier stopped separating agent from human flows. In real agent harnesses on a congested link, router queueing took 13% to 63% of task time, depending on harness and assumed model latency. The traffic was synthetic or sandbox-scale.

Abstract
LLMs often answer semantically equivalent queries inconsistently, even when the underlying facts are known. We formalize consistency as semantic invariance over equivalence classes of queries and show that invariance under arbitrary meaning-preserving transformations is undecidable in general. This motivates scoped semantic invariance, where model behavior is evaluated relative to bounded semantic domains. We operationalize this framework using knowledge graphs (KGs), which act as explicit semantic control structures defining local semantic constraints and grounding contexts. Across the MITRE D3FEND ontology and eight Wikidata- derived domains, we show that inconsistencies are structured rather than random, concentrating in localized hotspot subgraphs organized around recurring TREE, STAR, and CYCLE patterns that remain stable across models and domains. We further show that selective graph grounding improves correctness and cross-model consistency, while indiscriminate grounding introduces regressions. A layered grounding strategy achieves the strongest monotonicity profile, preserving correct knowledge while reducing inconsistency. Finally, we show that hotspot structures discovered from simple consistency probes transfer to more realistic compositional reasoning tasks.

Abstract
Knowledge graphs often contain missing or inconsistent facts that undermine the applications built on them. Schema validation detects such violations but does not identify which of several valid repairs recovers the intended graph. External corpora can supply the evidence needed to identify the intended repair, but retrieving from them is costly and not always successful. We propose a framework for selective retrieval in iterative graph repair. An LLM repair agent explores and edits the graph, while a router controls its access to a researcher agent that retrieves external evidence. Abstract
Large language models have demonstrated strong capabilities in structured reasoning and code generation, yet identifying an assembly fault does not guarantee the ability to generate executable rework programs. In interlocking assemblies, a locally plausible repair program may violate blocking dependencies, omit required recovery operations, or terminate in an incorrect configuration. We identify this challenge as fault-conditioned executable rework generation, where models must jointly reason about structural failures and generate programs that restore constrained assembly states. To benchmark this capability, we introduce FORGE-Bench, containing 4,000 tasks across 26 interlocking assemblies with five assemblies held out for evaluating generalization to unseen structures. We further propose FORGE (Fault-Oriented Rework Code Generation for Executable Interlocking Assembly), a structured state-to-program framework that decomposes rework generation into Rework Structuring and Action Grounding. Rework Structuring captures fault-induced recovery dependencies, while Action Grounding maps these dependencies into state-conditioned executable actions. Experiments show that locally reasonable repair code is insufficient for reliable assembly recovery, and that connecting structural recovery reasoning with executable action realization is essential for verified target restoration.

Abstract
Formal verification using proof assistants provides strong correctness guarantees for software, but constructing proofs remains labor-intensive and requires significant expertise. Recent approaches based on large language models (LLMs) have shown promise in automating proof synthesis. However, these approaches typically constrain the model to a rigid tactic-generation loop, in which the surrounding system controls premise retrieval, proof-state management, and backtracking. We introduce ProofCoder, an agentic proof-synthesis system for Rocq that approaches theorem proving from a coding-agent perspective. Rather than generating tactics within a fixed loop, ProofCoder reasons continuously throughout proof search and uses proof-assistant feedback to decide when to gather additional information, inspect proof states, revise previously generated proof fragments, or backtrack to an earlier point in the proof. This allows it to iteratively construct, repair, and refine proofs, at either tactic-level or whole-proof granularity. We evaluate ProofCoder on 280 challenging verification tasks from the AutoRocq Benchmark. On the best-performing configurations, ProofCoder increases proof success from 47% to 60% over the state-of-theart AutoRocq system, corresponding to a 27.7% relative improvement at approximately half the cost. Our ablations identify continuous reasoning as a key design choice: without it, the agent solves fewer lemmas at substantially higher cost, backtracks four to six times more often, and more frequently discards its own revisions. Whole-proof and tactic-level generation achieve similar success rates but follow distinct search and repair strategies. These results suggest that effective automated software verification requires more than improved tactic selection: continuous reasoning and feedback-guided proof revision are critical ingredients for robust proof synthesis.

Abstract
Language-model agents increasingly tackle long-horizon tasks whose interaction histories exceed the model's active context. They must therefore decide what to write down and what to read back, and when to do either. Recent work has begun to use on-policy distillation (OPD) to make memory control part of the policy, relying on memory tools and compaction protocols that lie outside either model's pre-training. To study this problem, we introduce Coding Agent Memory Gym II (CAMG-II), a suite of long-horizon agentic-RL environments spanning Shop, Coding, AutoResearch, General, and Visual. On CAMG-II, CAMP-II obtains 2.97 times the gain of the strongest OPD baseline. The memory behavior CAMP-II learns generalizes to two external benchmarks, Terminal-Bench 2.1 and SWE-Bench Pro V2, with improved success rates.

Abstract
Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG) faces significant challenges in complex relational retrieval tasks, particularly in densely connected knowledge graphs where the number of possible paths grows rapidly with reasoning depth. Existing GraphRAG retrieval algorithms either rely on rigid, predefined heuristics to identify relevant graph context or employ costly LLM-based reasoning to guide graph traversal. Abstract
LLM services have become essential infrastructure for applications such as chat assistants and coding agents. Service providers need to attribute resource consumption to individual requests for purposes such as billing. Among these resources, energy is a key metric for assessing the cost of serving each request. However, serving engines batch requests together, and GPUs report only aggregate energy consumption. Providers typically estimate each request's share in proportion to its token consumption, but this approach can produce substantially unfair estimates. The Shapley value provides a principled solution to fair energy allocation, but computing it directly requires $2^N-1$ GPU runs for $N$ requests, which is infeasible for large batches. To address this problem, we propose StepSplit. Across GPU generations, we observe that the energy consumed by each inference step, a forward pass over a batch, consists of a shared energy, largely due to reading model weights, and a request-specific energy. We derive a closed-form Shapley value: each request is charged its own energy plus an equal part of the shared energy of every step in which it participates. A microbenchmark of 2.5-17.8 minutes calibrates both parts of the step energy once per model and GPU configuration. Under static batching, StepSplit uses this closed form in a single pass over the scheduler log. Under continuous batching, removing a request can change how the remaining requests are batched, so StepSplit adds a correction to the closed form, computed by replaying the scheduler on a CPU. We evaluate StepSplit against Shapley values measured using 529,513 GPU runs. Under static batching with batch sizes of 8-256, StepSplit achieves an $L_1$ error of 0.4-4.3%, compared with 13.4-61.3% for token-proportional allocation. Under continuous batching with 8-128 requests, the corresponding errors are 0.5-6.4% and 20.6-71.7%.

Abstract
Classical planners can effectively solve very large deterministic MDPs represented in STRIPS or PDDL where states are sets of atoms over objects and relations, and lifted action schemas add or delete these atoms. This compact representation yields strong search heuristics and provides an ideal setting for structural generalization, since lifted relations and action schemas give rise to infinitely many domain instances. A central challenge is to learn these relations and action schemas from data, and recent approaches have addressed this problem using different types of observations. In this work, we develop a novel neural network architecture for learning action schemas from traces where states are fully observed but action arguments are unobserved. Additionally, we report experiments on robustness to observation noise, on component ablations, and on a variation related to slot-based dynamics models.

Abstract
When language model agents tackle complex software engineering tasks, they often degrade over long trajectories, which we define as *agent drift*. We focus on two recurring failure modes *overthinking* and *overacting*, i.e., where the agent repeatedly reasons over information it already has, and where it issues tool calls without integrating recent observations or acquiring new evidence. In this paper, we introduce TACT (Think-Act Calibration via activation Steering), to detect and mitigate agent drift in the residual stream before it surfaces as a behavioral failure. In specific, we label trajectory steps as overthinking, overacting, or calibrated, and find that their hidden states can separate linearly along two *drift axes*, pointing from calibrated behavior toward each failure mode (AUC $\approx$ 0.9). To mitigate agent drift, we project each step's activation onto these axes at test time and pull drifted ones back toward the calibrated region. These gains frame agent drift as a steerable direction in the residual stream, and position TACT as a viable handle for reliable long-horizon agents.

Abstract
Amid the rapid advancement of physical-world intelligence, cloud-edge collaborative large language models (LLMs) have emerged as a promising roadmap for practical LLM deployment. However, existing cloud-edge paradigms struggle to balance global consensus with local personalization, which fails to satisfy the need for a unified knowledge foundation on the cloud and domain-specific adaptation at the edge. To address this, we introduce, for the first time, a personalized bilevel optimization framework that formalizes cloud-edge LLM collaboration as a dual structure: the upper level optimizes edge-side personalization, while the lower level governs cloud-side knowledge transfer, reaching cloud-edge evolving in coordination. We then propose CIDERS, an efficient solver that decomposes the model into a learnable backbone and a messenger. While the cloud performs knowledge transfer to the learnable backbone, the key lies in embedding global trajectories into each local personalization step via consensus-variate correction to reconcile personalization with consensus. We provide a comprehensive theoretical analysis, including a geometric characterization of the local trajectory and a full convergence guarantee, revealing an explicit trade-off structure between personalization and global convergence. Extensive experiments demonstrate that CIDERS consistently outperforms competitive baselines on the compressed edge path, with 3.1x and 1.7x gains on mathematical reasoning and code generation, respectively, and a 10\% relative gain on instruction metrics. Mechanism experiments attribute these gains to early consensus-corrected coordination and task-aware distillation. Abstract
Large language model agents are increasingly deployed as multi-agent systems rather than as a standalone model operating within a single context. Recent agent harnesses allow the model to instantiate worker agents and to author the executable workflow that coordinates them, shifting the design of system structure from developers to the model at run time. The quality of this organization therefore constitutes a distinct capability, yet existing benchmarks either measure end-to-end task completion or evaluate coordination under structures specified in advance. We formalize this capability as workflow orchestration and introduce WorkflowArena, a benchmark of 145 long-horizon tasks spanning 8 professional domains, from software engineering and formal mathematics to legal analysis and 3D production. Each task specifies an initial workspace and an integrated deliverable composed of interdependent components, which is assessed by a hidden verifier. Agents determine autonomously whether and how to delegate, and all delegation is recorded, enabling joint evaluation of deliverable quality and the organization of the underlying work. Experiments with nine models show that no single configuration performs best across all domains and that systems exhibit distinct delegation strategies: at high effort, Claude models in Claude Code invoke workflows less frequently but with larger worker groups, whereas GPT models in Codex delegate more frequently in smaller units. When the executing model is held fixed on a challenging subset of tasks, workflows authored by Claude Opus 5.5 substantially improve weaker executors, indicating that workflow organization is itself a measurable determinant of task performance.

Abstract
Modern cyber threats commonly unfold as multi-step attacks comprising temporally and causally related phases. Current defenses commonly follow a detect first and correlate later paradigm. Intrusion detection systems classify events independently, while downstream systems reconstruct attack progression from the resulting alerts or audit records. In this context, learning-based detectors learn attack-step dependencies implicitly as statistical regularities in the data and cannot guarantee that their predictions follow the real attack progression. Conversely, correlation systems represent attack relationships explicitly, but apply this knowledge only after the underlying attack observations have been detected. We present DEEPCHRONOS, a neuro-symbolic network intrusion detection system that shifts the paradigm from detect first and correlate later to detection through logical progression. DEEPCHRONOS combines learned flow-level evidence with explicit attack-phase rules, temporal prerequisites, and previously inferred phases, allowing the evolving attack history to inform and constrain each detection decision. Evaluations on DARPA 2000 and AIT-LDS V2 show that DEEPCHRONOS improves macro F1 over purely neural baselines by up to 11.3%, eliminates causal violations under strict temporal constraints, and maintains millisecond-scale inference latency. These results demonstrate that integrating explicit attack progression knowledge into detection produces more accurate and temporally coherent multi-step attack predictions.

Abstract
Large Language Model (LLM) code agents increasingly resolve repository-level issues by iteratively editing code, invoking tools, and validating candidate patches. In these workflows, agents often write tests on the fly, but the value of this behavior remains unclear. This raises a central question: how does agent-written testing relate to task outcomes and resource use during repository-level issue resolution? To better understand the role of agent-written tests, we analyze 5,000 recorded agent runs produced by six strong LLMs on SWE-bench Verified using the lightweight mini-SWE-agent scaffold. Our results show that test writing is common for most models. GPT-5.2 is an exception: it rarely writes new test files while achieving a resolution rate close to the top-ranked model. Test-writing frequency varies widely across models. Within a given model, it differs less between resolved and unresolved tasks. When tests are written, their files contain substantially more statements that print runtime values than assertions that check conditions. We then compare baseline prompts with revised prompts that encourage or discourage writing new test files for four models. Agents start or stop writing test files as requested on 36.8%-93.2% of tasks. Resolved-task totals differ by 0-13 out of 500 per model, with no statistically significant difference, while API calls or token use differ substantially for three models. Similar resolution totals can therefore hide substantial differences in how agents test and how many resources they use.

Abstract
We propose SciMARS, a framework for recursive task-local adaptation of multi-agent harnesses in closed-loop scientific discovery. Multi-agent systems coordinate analysis, experimental design, and verification as scientific evidence evolves. However, two problems limit their effectiveness: (i) without shared memory, agents lose observations and repeat work; (ii) fixed role procedures, handoffs, and memory policies perpetuate mistakes despite retained evidence. We address these problems with a shared evidence graph and separate component editors. Abstract
A core obstacle to alignment evaluation is evaluation awareness: capable models can tell when they are being tested rather than deployed, weakening the conclusions a safety evaluation can support. We present two techniques that make simulated alignment evaluations harder to distinguish from real deployments. Our first technique, critique refinement, spends additional inference-time compute on each simulator action: the simulator generates multiple candidate actions, refines them using feedback from an instance of the target model on how to make them more realistic, and continues the evaluation with the most deployment-like candidate. Our second technique, DISH (Deployment-Imitating SWE-Agent Harness), wraps the target in an agent harness, reducing the gap between simulated and real deployment environments in coding settings. We test the techniques on multiple target models and find that applying both yields larger realism gains than either alone. We show that automated approaches can improve the realism of alignment evaluations, and that they compare favorably to spending the extra compute on more audit attempts.

Abstract
Every major LLM agent framework gives the LLM the role of orchestrator; the model decides what to do next, when to call tools, and when to stop. We argue that token explosion, control-flow hallucination, and unreliable completion are not implementation bugs but architectural consequences of assigning the deterministic work of looping, branching, and sequencing to a probabilistic system. A better prompt or a stronger model cannot guarantee the reliability of the LLM agent. We therefore propose Agentic Programming, in which the program governs all control flow, and the LLM is itself part of it, an adaptive component we call LLM-as-Code and invoke only where a task calls for reasoning or generation. Within each call the model keeps full flexibility, but it cannot alter the program's execution path. With control in the program, the LLM's context is built from the execution history's call tree and forms a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Each call's context length is then determined by its call depth rather than by accumulation over steps. Abstract
Code-graph and embedding tools are routinely added to LLM coding agents on the premise that they supply repository structure the model lacks. We report a pre-registered study of how much such tools help off-the-shelf agents localize the code an issue requires changing and how the benefit varies with model size. Nine open-weight models (Qwen3 0.6B to 32B, Gemma 4 E2B to 12B) run one fixed localization harness on 500 held-out SWE-bench tasks under four tool sets (grep only; plus embedding search; plus code-graph tools; both) and a placebo arm whose structural tools return random entities, with five sampled seeds per condition: 170,500 episodes under hypotheses, tests and claim criteria frozen before the runs. The tools raise canonical function Acc@10 by +0.06 to +0.10 for the Qwen3 models up to 14B and the two small Gemma 4 models, by +0.03 at 32B (a near miss after correction), and not at all for Gemma 4 12B, which rarely submits; the gain is larger for small models than for the largest model in both families. The placebo shows that the gain is caused by what the tools return rather than by having them, and that random tool output harms two small models. The pre-registered claim that a 4B model with tools matches a 32B model without them fails. Secondary analyses show that the embedding index, not the code graph, carries the gain; that the gain replicates on tasks created after every model's release and survives a shell harness, thinking mode, FP8 serving and a file-level metric that output formatting cannot inflate; and that it is mostly exposure: the tools show the gold code more often. Injecting the gold code directly explains the size trend. A 32B model already finds the code without the tools, so they add little exposure, while small models act on a shown answer less often (conversion 0.09, 0.24 and 0.40 at 1.7B, 4B and 32B). We release registration, code, episodes and analyses.

Abstract
Correlation Matrix Memories (CMMs) store associations as sums of rank-one outer products, providing closed-form addressability, exact deletion, and linear attribution. While classical analyses assume random, orthogonal keys, semantic encoders produce correlated representations. We analyse CMMs under correlated keys using frame-theoretic coherence: (i) capacity bounds yielding an encoder-independent limit $n \le d(1+\varepsilon^2)$ for mean squared crosstalk $\varepsilon^2$; (ii) an exact characterisation of the collateral effect of deleting an association, guaranteeing that deletion never invalidates certified associations; and (iii) a geometric transparency bound demonstrating why attribution requires candidate sets of centred keys. On this basis, we introduce Topology-Aware Neuro-Symbolic Associative Retrieval (TANSAR). TANSAR traverses a directed acyclic association graph, prunes invalid paths via sound ground Horn constraints, and injects a representation derived strictly from the verified path into the residual stream of a frozen language model, guarded by a Clopper--Pearson selective-risk gate. Controlled experiments confirm all theoretical bounds. In multi-hop knowledge editing, Horn pruning reduces answer contradictions from 25.1% to 0.5%, whereas Hopfield retrieval incurs 24% contradictions when stale associations remain stored (and 0% if stale associations are removed). An end-to-end edit transaction requires 13.6 ms on a standard CPU and 2.37 ms on GPU. The symbolic component matches gold labels across all 600 ProofWriter theories and achieves 94.0% agreement on FOLIO, demonstrating sound and efficient multi-hop associative retrieval with exact unlearning.

Abstract
Action theories typically fix their vocabulary and causal laws, whereas agent harnesses can change their tools, providers, and policies during execution. We introduce Reconfigurable Action Theories (RAT), in which actions can change the action theory itself. A theory module in RAT contains a vocabulary, causal laws, and an initializer. A RAT configuration records the active modules and the world state. Module loading and unloading are represented by actions governed by ordinary causal laws. RAT retains the standard causal action semantics of its language fragment when the active module set is fixed. We give sufficient conditions for at most one next configuration, preservation of existing behavior when a module is added, and equal results when modules are loaded in either order. We establish the computational complexity of reasoning tasks in RAT and translate finite RAT systems into nonmonotonic causal theories and Answer Set Programming (ASP), preserving bounded executions. Experiments compare finite RAT models with executions of official DeepSeek Harness, Pi, and OpenHands implementations. RAT provides a formal basis for checking how component changes affect a harness's available actions, behavior, and reachable goals.

Abstract
Early successes on agentic robotics point to a potential paradigm shift toward using coding agents to control robots. Existing robotics benchmarks are built around visuomotor policy learning, leaving it open whether coding agents can reliably control diverse robots under physical-world constraints. We therefore introduce the Embodied First Exam, a unified, comprehensive, and agent-native evaluation framework where coding agents can choose their own control approach: direct control, code to control, or train a policy to control.
