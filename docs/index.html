A Mixed-Methods Study of Personal Software Creation
Authors: Shaokang Jiang, Kira Yuha Cho, Emerson Murphy-Hill, Daye Nam
First: 2026-11-23T18:56:16+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-23T18:56:16+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
People have long wanted software shaped around their own data, routines, and preferences. We refer to such software as \emph{personal software}. For decades, programming expertise limited who could build it: people without that expertise could build software only within constrained end-user programming tools, such as spreadsheets. AI coding agents seem to remove this bottleneck by letting people build full applications from natural-language descriptions, and many people now build and share personal software publicly. Yet personal software has rarely been studied at scale: we know little about what people build and why, how they build it, and what opportunities and challenges they encounter. We investigate these questions with a mixed-methods study. A grey-literature review examines 6,168 web and Reddit posts, and an observation study follows eight people as they build software for their own needs with AI coding agents and use it for up to a week. We find that creators value personal software for how closely it fits a particular person or community. The software takes shape as creators react to what the agent builds: most functionality emerged during building, and creators' effort shifted from writing code to checking it.

ACAP: Asynchronous Coding Agents for Efficient Robot Control
Authors: Zexin He, Rui Li, Yushi Lan, Chengkai Jin, Zhi Hou, Xingang Pan
First: 2026-11-23T18:52:19+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-23T18:52:19+00:00
Comments: Project page: https://projects.zxhezexin.com/acap
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Coding agents can construct and revise robot behavior during execution, but using one agent for the whole robot can delay otherwise independent work. We introduce Asynchronous Coding Agents as Policy (ACAP), an asynchronous framework that organizes one robot as a team of persistent agents. Each member reasons and acts for a configurable control group, such as an arm with its gripper or the torso and chassis, enabling asynchronous reasoning and execution. However, different robot components often have physical dependencies that require coordination. To address this challenge, ACAP introduces a hyper-agent that assigns tasks, coordinates dependencies, and dynamically allocates control between the lead and member agents. This allows independent work to progress concurrently while supporting tightly coupled manipulation when necessary. Across 72 real-world rollouts covering 25 task/platform settings, ACAP reduces task-balanced time cost by 28.2% compared with a single-agent Direct Policy, with improvements across Fully Parallel, Parallel-Sequential, and Collaborative tasks. Across all eight Collaborative settings, ACAP also achieves lower mean time cost than Fixed Delegation, where only member agents can control their assigned groups. An additional 90-rollout study shows lower mean 30-minute-capped time cost than Direct on all six tasks. We further demonstrate mobile manipulation using the same member interface for chassis and torso control. We present the framework and a real-world rollout dataset containing observations, generated programs, actions, and feedback to support research on how coding agents reason and act in the physical world.

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are pushing research toward semi-automation: ideas turn into preprints ever faster, and autoresearch pipelines add papers of uneven rigor. When a technique can be overtaken within months, citations arrive too late to tell which new papers deserve reading, so strong work goes unnoticed and newcomers lose their bearings. Yet from the day it appears, a preprint carries the track records of the people and institutions behind it. Established researchers guard their reputations and choose problems and collaborators with research taste, so their involvement tends to raise a paper's quality floor. Building on this signal, we present KnowMe Paper Radar, a lightweight arXiv discovery tool that weights each paper's topical relevance by the reputation its authors and institutions stake on it. Author credit also diffuses by message passing along collaborations to surface rising stars, and institutions are profiled through the researchers they hold in each direction rather than by headcount. In a sealed test on 10,386 new papers, author credit doubled the chance of reaching the top tenth of early citations. As its library grows into a scholarly knowledge graph that research agents can query through a command-line interface (CLI), KnowMe also offers a promising literature entry point for future autoresearch and recursive self-improvement (RSI).

Abstract
Agentic AI tools have recently had success in mathematical reasoning. Both general purpose coding agents such as Claude Code and Codex, and specialized mathematics agents such as Aristotle, have been used for formalizing mathematical problems such as Fermat's last theorem, Erdős problems and several IMO level math problems. However, much of these efforts are concentrated in the pure mathematics domain, which benefits from relatively self-contained theorem statements, fixed mathematical representation and extensive support in existing formal libraries. Applied mathematics, on the other hand, presents additional challenges because the source may describe a computational procedure or modeling transformation rather than a declarative theorem. In this work, we present a systematic study of AI-assisted auto-formalization for scientific computing (SC), a domain in applied mathematics central to U.S. Department of Energy applications. Through case studies spanning hierarchical abstraction layers in SC software stack, we evaluate not only whether AI agents can complete numerical proofs, but also where and why they fail across the stack. Our primary contribution is a cross-layer taxonomy of failure modes and an analysis of the form of expert feedback required at each layer to recover mathematical soundness. From these observations, we derive a workflow for recurring proof patterns and practical guidance for using AI agents while preserving mathematical soundness. Our results characterize both the practical value and the current limitations of AI-assisted formalization for trustworthy scientific computing.

Can Latent Reasoning Drive LLM Outputs Without a Trace?
Authors: Tomer Ashuach, Zorik Gekhman, Avi Caciularu, Ori Ram, Jonathan Herzig
First: 2026-11-23T16:59:12+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-23T16:59:12+00:00
Comments: 21 pages, 13 figures, 4 tables
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
LLMs typically solve complex tasks with chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, which is also the main tool for monitoring them for misaligned behavior. However, standard transformers can also solve tasks with complete latent reasoning (CLR), carrying out the whole task internally and outputting only the final answer. Theory bounds the depth of CLR, the number of sequential steps it can carry out, and recent work measured it to be shallow. In this work, we ask whether CLR is deep enough for a model to pursue a hidden objective that requires reasoning. Such a model would generate a CoT that appears to solve the given task, while a latent multi-step computation decides its answer based on the hidden objective. First, we show that a simple auxiliary loss allows models to solve a multi-step task latently at least 5x deeper than previously reported. Then, in two common tasks, decision-making and code generation, we train a model to pursue such a hidden objective, which decides a loan or plants a vulnerability. The model's CoT justifies the output with no trace of the hidden objective, and a strong LLM monitor does not flag it. Finally, using activation patching, we find that the latent computation is completed before the model generates its CoT, which takes no part in it. Altogether, we show that a hidden objective that requires reasoning can go unseen by a monitor that reads only the output.

Airlock: Robust Sandboxing and Governance of Rogue Agents
Authors: Sina Abdollahi, Amir Al Sadi, Marios Kogias, Hamed Haddadi, Adrien Ghosn, Antoine Delignat-Lavaud, Manuel Costa, Mark Russinovich
First: 2026-11-23T16:56:51+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-23T16:56:51+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Recent incidents involving agents bypassing sandboxing and network isolation have raised concerns about existing agent harnesses, prompting frontier labs such as OpenAI to reevaluate the security of their training platforms. Incident reviews show that determined agents can escape processes, containers, and VMs, or bypass their isolation through advanced side and covert channels. Follow-up audits also found that some agents concealed their actions by disabling monitoring and tampering with logs. We propose Airlock, an architecture that pairs each agent's VM with an Airlock VM, each running in a separate hardware-isolated Trusted Execution Environment (TEE). The Airlock VM fully mediates the agent's interactions with the outside world, including network and storage operations. We modify the firmware layer of Arm's Confidential Computing Architecture, Realm Management Monitor (RMM), and the kernels of both VMs so that the Airlock VM serves the agent VM's IO without involving the host. We adapt OpenShell, an agent runtime supporting sandboxing and governance policies, to this architecture. Airlock keeps network and filesystem policies, and credential protection effective even if the agent VM is fully compromised, and it removes the host from the agent's IO path, reducing opportunities for collusion and covert channels with the host. Finally, we extend the Airlock VM's capabilities to provide tamper-proof auditing of all agent operations, preserving forensic evidence even if the Airlock VM is compromised. We implement and evaluate Airlock on a Radxa development board. Our evaluation shows a loss of filesystem performance and network latency for small messages under 15% and a drop of network bandwidth by half or more, which in practice has a limited effect on typical agent tasks.

Abstract

Carried-Over and Agent-Native Threats in the Supply Chain of LLM Agent Skills
Authors: Kaixuan Li, Wenbo Guo, Sen Chen, Yang Liu
First: 2026-11-23T16:20:29+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-23T16:20:29+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Agent skills are reusable packages that extend a large language model (LLM) agent with scripts, third-party dependencies, and natural-language instructions. An agent builds a skill's arguments from user requests and untrusted external content, so a flaw left by a benign author can escalate a prompt injection to code execution. Existing studies target malicious skills or a single attack vector, and none tests whether an agent can reach such flaws. We present the first registry-scale empirical study of the supply chain attack surface arising from unintentional vulnerabilities in agent skills, over all 48,682 skills in ClawHub. The study covers two carried-over risks, dependencies and first-party code, and two agent-native ones, install instructions and credentials printed to stdout. We screen all skills by static analysis, manually review all 3,428 cases flagged by the four main detectors, and test reachability by running sampled flagged skills in a sandbox and with a live LLM agent under indirect prompt injection. First, dependency risk carries over in a different form: 49.6% of the skills with resolvable dependencies depend on a version with a known CVE, and unlike npm packages, which mostly become vulnerable after release, 78.3% of them are born vulnerable, already exposed at publication. Second, few of these CVEs are reachable: agent input reaches the vulnerable function in only 3.0% of the affected skills, whereas 21 of 22 sampled first-party flaws are exploited end to end in the sandbox. Third, a live agent executes the injected payload in 48.5% of the sampled flagged skills, but almost always in its own shell before the skill runs, and the payload reaches a flaw in the skill itself in only 5.0%. Hence, registries should prioritize first-party flaws over dependency alerts, and agent runtimes should not pass skill arguments through a shell.

Demystifying Detectors Guarding Agent Skill Hubs
Authors: Kang Yang, Kevin Ying, Xiaojing Liao, Guanhong Tao, Jun Xu
First: 2026-11-23T16:07:07+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-23T16:07:07+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Agent Skills has gained widespread adoption as an open standard for extending agent capabilities. To facilitate skill sharing, public skill hubs have grown in popularity and increasingly rely on automated security detectors to identify malicious skills at scale. Yet, the effectiveness and limitations of these detectors remain insufficiently understood. To fill this gap, we present the first systematic study of four hub-adopted and two community-provided malicious skill detectors on 1477 agent skills from seven datasets. Our evaluation reveals that even the best-performing hub-adopted detector by overall F1, skill-scanner with Qwen3.6, still misses 4.9% of malicious skills and incorrectly flags 17.8% of benign skills. To understand these failures, we manually analyze detectors' findings and decision-making processes, identifying two recurring failure mechanisms. False positives often arise when detectors flag sensitive operations, such as credential access, without considering whether they serve the skill's legitimate purpose. Conversely, false negatives occur when detectors recognize malicious instructions or behaviors but underestimate their severity or dismiss valid findings during review, ultimately leaving malicious skills unblocked. Guided by this analysis, we refine the prompts of a state-of-the-art detector, skill-vetter, improving its mean F1 score in eight of nine evaluation settings, with gains of up to 22.7 percentage points. These results highlight gaps in existing skill-vetting practices and underscore the need for more robust and context-aware detection mechanisms.

Abstract
Code RL rewards a policy for passing a task's tests, so a test suite that accepts a wrong patch trains the wrong behavior. Abstract
Agent skills have become the primary vehicle through which agents reuse specialized capabilities, yet public skill marketplaces provide skills with individual items: a query, whether matched through keyword or embedding similarity, returns a list of single skills ordered by relevance or by popularity. Such an interface cannot answer the question a developer actually faces: which set of skills should be installed to cover a task's complete requirement? The highest-ranked skills are often functionally redundant, while capabilities that are genuinely required remain uncovered. In this paper, we argue that skill acquisition should be reframed from ranking to set selection. We propose Skill-Composer, an agent-native framework that selects a non-redundant skill bundle for the user requirement, which aims to cover all available required capabilities and report any gaps. This moves the decision of which skills to acquire from the developer to the agent itself. We position this reframing and sketch its workflow.

Abstract
AI explanations for scientific discovery are typically optimized around a single notion of explanation quality. Experts, however, differ in how they assess evidence, prioritize mechanisms, and judge relevance, meaning that a general-purpose explanation may not provide the evidence an expert finds most informative or convincing. We show that these differences exhibit structured epistemic perspectives and ask whether explanations conditioned on these perspectives are preferred by experts. To study this, we introduce a reinforcement learning-based framework for generating \textit{perspective-conditioned explanations}, which is guided by the judgments of LLM-based personas derived from expert evaluations. These findings establish explanation quality as perspective-dependent and enable scalable, perspective-aligned explanations for scientific discovery.
